One name, in the wrong book

Three hundred years ago a clerk's apprentice made a filing error. The book has been correcting it ever since, one bewildered stranger at a time.

The apprentice

His name is Smudge. He is an otter, he is roughly three hundred years into a job that was advertised as temporary, and he has never once been thanked for it.

The job was simple: copy names into the ledger. Names in, names recorded, nothing else required. He was good at it for almost a week. Then came an evening with poor light, a blunt quill and a name that could reasonably have been spelled two ways, and Smudge picked the wrong one.

Nothing happened. That was the worrying part.

The ledger

The book did not complain. It did not correct itself. It simply decided, quietly and permanently, that the error was now its responsibility to resolve — and it has been resolving it ever since by producing the people it thinks were meant to be written down.

It is not accurate. A ledger that was accurate would not have produced a Roman legionary who is afraid of water, a corgi who conquered most of Europe and still cannot reach the top shelf, or a cat who ruled Egypt and now refuses to walk anywhere she can be carried.

It is, however, persistent. Every time the circle fills, somebody else falls out of history and onto the floor of the cellar, and every one of them arrives mid-sentence, mid-argument, or mid-escape.

Why your typing matters

Here is the inconvenient mechanism nobody designed on purpose: the ledger runs on effort, and the nearest available source of effort is you.

Every key you press is one swing of the stamp, one tug on a fishing line, one turn of a lockpick. Smudge cannot read what you are writing and has never shown the slightest interest. He only feels the keys and gets on with the work. Answer your email and he fills the circle. Write a novel and he will have summoned half of recorded history before chapter four.

The desktop pet counts how many keys you press. It never records which ones.

Where the work happens

The cellar is called the Archive, and it is home. Everywhere else is a hole in time that Smudge has learned to climb through: a Roman dock, an ice hole in Lofoten, a Venetian vault, a speakeasy safe, and an office supply closet that has defeated better thieves than him.

Each one has its own trade and its own tide of lost property, because when somebody falls out of history, whatever they were carrying falls out after them — just not necessarily in the same century. See all nine places.

The arrivals

Thirty-six strangers so far, and the book is nowhere near finished. Each one is a card: a life, a set of numbers, and the three things they were holding when the ledger took an interest in them.

None of them asked to be here. Most of them have opinions about it. Meet all the arrivals.
